About Deepak Goyal

I work for traders. Most of my files come out of the wholesale markets — Loha Mandi, Belanganj, the shoe units, the transport and commission agents — and most of them start the same way: goods went out, a cheque came in, and the cheque came back. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act is what that becomes, and filing it properly is largely a question of dates.

Those dates are unforgiving. The legal demand notice has to go within thirty days of the bank return memo, the drawer gets fifteen days to pay, and the complaint has to be filed within one month of that period ending. Miss any of them and a perfectly good claim is gone on limitation, which is the single most common reason a trader with an honest case loses it. So the first thing my chamber does with a returned cheque is put the calendar on paper. I also defend accused parties in 138 matters, where the questions are quite different — whether the cheque was a security cheque, whether the debt was legally enforceable, whether the notice was served at all.

The commercial truth about this work is that a trader wants the money, not a conviction. A large share of these matters are best resolved by compounding on agreed terms, and I will negotiate that wherever the other side is willing to pay, because a settlement in the ninth month is worth more than an order in the fourth year. Where recovery is larger than a cheque or the dealing was on credit without an instrument, the route is a money recovery suit or an MSME reference instead, and I will tell you which one your papers actually support.
